You've found the apartment, agreed the contract, and the landlord is waiting for money: the deposit (*kaucja*) — a median of about 5 000 zł in Warsaw (Passflat tenant data, August 2026) — plus the first month's rent. Eight or nine thousand złoty that must change hands *before* you move in. And here's the trap: the money is due now, but you have no Polish account yet — no PESEL, no bank visit behind you, and a transfer from your home bank is expensive, slow, or simply impossible.

The good news: this is solvable in a few days. The catch: the right path depends on your legal status, and there is no universal "just get Wise" answer.

## Three scenarios: what actually works for your status

- **You have UKR status, a karta pobytu, or a national D visa.** Wise or Revolut will most likely verify you without trouble. This is the fastest first-week option: a working account in a day or two, no PESEL, no branch visit.
- **You arrived recently and don't yet have a document confirming legal stay** — a common situation for citizens of Belarus or Russia in their first weeks. Let's be blunt: Wise and Revolut verification often *fails* in this case — EEA-residency requirements were tightened in December 2025. Your realistic path: pay the kaucja in cash against a signed receipt (*pokwitowanie*), or with your home bank's card if it works in Poland, then open a Polish account in a branch as soon as you have a PESEL or meldunek.
- **You want a Polish bank straight away.** Online applications with selfie verification often stumble on foreign passports. The reliable route is a branch: with a passport (plus karta pobytu if you have one) they'll open the account in person — at some banks even without a PESEL.

Requirements shift every few months — check the current terms on the bank's website before any step.

## Wise: an account in a couple of days — if it verifies you

If your status lets you pass verification (a document confirming legal stay plus a selfie), [Wise](https://wise.com) covers almost the entire first month:

- You top up by card or by transfer from your home bank and hold the money in złoty — conversion at the mid-market rate with a transparent fee, and Wise gives you account details for receiving transfers.
- You send the kaucja and rent as an ordinary bank transfer to the landlord's Polish account — within Poland it usually arrives the same day.
- In the transfer title you write "kaucja", the address, and the date — leaving a paper trail, which matters more than it seems (more below).

There's one real limitation: Wise is a bridge, not a replacement for a Polish bank. For salary, utility settlements, and everyday life you'll still need a Polish account — you just get to open it calmly instead of in a panic.

## Opening an account in a branch: what to bring and how long it takes

Why a branch? An online application with a selfie is an automated document check trained mostly on Polish IDs. It often fails to read a foreign passport: the application hangs "in verification" or gets rejected with no explanation. In a branch, a human looks at your document instead.

What to bring:

- **Your passport** — the core document; at most banks it's enough.
- **Karta pobytu or visa**, if you have one — removes almost every question.
- **PESEL, if already issued** — with it, the account opens in 30–60 minutes; without it, opening is still possible, but the procedure takes longer and not every branch is used to it.
- **Your rental contract** — sometimes requested as proof of address.

Typical timeline: with a PESEL — account, card in the app, and working BLIK the same day; without one — anywhere from an hour to a couple of days. Practice varies between branches: if one turns you down, try another branch or another bank — that's normal, not a sign you're "not eligible".

## The kaucja: how to hand over money you'll want back

Whatever method you use, the rule is the same: **whenever possible, pay by bank transfer**. A transfer is evidence in itself: amount, date, recipient. In the transfer title, put "kaucja za mieszkanie", the apartment's address, and the date.

If a transfer is impossible and you pay cash — only against a receipt (*pokwitowanie*): amount, date, address, the word "kaucja", and both signatures. Without paper, getting the deposit back turns into your word against theirs.

Getting it back is realistic, by the way: 77% of Warsaw tenants who moved out recovered their deposit in full (Passflat tenant data, August 2026). ## How much to have ready for the first month

Count the full move-in cost, not just the rent: a deposit of around 5 000 zł at the median, plus the first month's rent, plus — on top of rent — Warsaw tenants pay a median of about 800 zł more per month in czynsz administracyjny, utilities, and settlements (Passflat tenant data, August 2026). For a typical apartment that means 10–12 thousand złoty in the first weeks. The full price picture by district is in our [Warsaw rent price report](/en/blog/warsaw-rent-prices).

One last thing. Before you transfer five thousand złoty to anyone, [check the building's address](/en/warsaw/check) — tenants may already have shared real costs and deposit-return experience for that exact building. And once the first bills arrive, [add your own costs](/en/warsaw/costs/submit): two minutes, no account needed, and the next newcomer will have it easier than you did.
