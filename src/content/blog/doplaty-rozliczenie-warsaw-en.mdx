Six months into your Warsaw tenancy, your landlord forwards a photo of a document from the building administration. It's called a *rozliczenie* — a settlement — and at the bottom there's a line: "do dopłaty: 1 450 zł". You owe another 1 450 zł, on top of everything you've paid so far. Is this a scam?

Almost certainly not. The surprise top-up bill — the *dopłata* — is built into how Polish utility billing works, and sooner or later every tenant meets one. But you should never pay it blind. Here's the full anatomy of a Warsaw rent bill, how the settlement system works, and exactly how to check that the number you've been handed is fair.

## The three layers of a Warsaw rent bill

What you actually pay each month is a stack of three different things, and listings usually advertise only the first:

- **Czynsz najmu** — the base rent, the part that goes to the landlord. This is the number in the listing.
- **Czynsz administracyjny** — the administrative fee the apartment owes to the housing cooperative (*spółdzielnia*) or owners' association (*wspólnota*). It covers building maintenance, rubbish collection, and — crucially — usually contains the monthly advances for water and often heating.
- **Media** — utilities billed by consumption: electricity (*prąd*), gas (*gaz*), and whatever isn't inside the administrative fee.

The dopłata lives in layers two and three, and to understand it you need one more concept.

## Zaliczka and rozliczenie: the surprise is built into the system

In Poland you don't pay utilities as you use them. You pay fixed monthly advances — each one a *zaliczka* — for electricity, gas, water and heating. A zaliczka is a forecast of your consumption, not your actual consumption.

Every 6–12 months comes the *rozliczenie*: the provider (or the building administration) reads the meters and compares what you actually used against what you paid in advances.

- Used more than you paid in? You get a **dopłata** — a top-up bill.
- Used less? The overpayment (*nadpłata*) is refunded or credited against future advances.

Who settles with whom matters. Electricity and gas usually go directly through the provider — PGE, Tauron, PGNiG. Water and heating usually settle **through the building**: the advances sit inside the czynsz administracyjny, and the reconciliation is done by the spółdzielnia or wspólnota. That's why the water-and-heating dopłata arrives not as a letter from a utility company but as a document from the building administration, passed on by your landlord. Which meters can go in your name and how the transfer works is covered in our [step-by-step meter guide](/en/blog/przepisanie-licznikow-najem).

Hence the classic scenario: a year of quiet, then one settlement covering the whole period. The more the advances were underestimated, the bigger the final number.

## The dopłata: is it legit, and how to check

Start with your contract. If the *umowa najmu* (rental agreement) says the tenant covers media by actual consumption — and most Warsaw contracts do — then a dopłata for your period of tenancy is legitimate and you'll have to pay it. But "your period" and "your meters" are precisely the two things worth verifying.

### Four checks before you transfer anything

- **Ask for the settlement document itself.** Not a number in a chat — the actual rozliczenie from the administration or provider, showing the billing period, meter readings, tariff, and the advances already paid. A landlord with nothing to hide forwards it in a minute.
- **Compare meter readings.** The handover protocol (*protokół zdawczo-odbiorczy*) from your move-in day should record the starting readings. The difference between then and now is your consumption. If the settlement shows noticeably more, ask why.
- **Check the billing period against your tenancy.** Settlement covers January–December but you moved in July? You don't pay for the previous tenant's months — the amount should be split pro rata.
- **Check your advances were credited.** You've been paying zaliczki inside your monthly transfers all along — the settlement must subtract them. A landlord asking for a dopłata "on top" without showing where the advances went is the biggest red flag of all.

If the numbers hold up after these four checks, the dopłata is legitimate — pay it and move on. If they don't, you now have concrete arguments instead of a vague sense of being cheated.

## Protect yourself before you sign

The cheapest dopłata is the one you learn about before signing.

- **Ask for the apartment's last rozliczenie.** Was there a dopłata in the previous cycle, and how big? It's the best forecast of your future costs — better than any verbal assurance.
- **Record all meter readings in the handover protocol**, with timestamped photos. Without it, any dispute about consumption is word against word.
- **Ask what exactly sits inside the czynsz administracyjny** — whether heating and water are included or billed separately.

## Winter is when dopłaty get big

The biggest variable in any settlement is heating. The gap between buildings is enormous: an old *kamienica* (pre-war tenement) with a gas water heater in each flat can burn through 300–600 zł a month in winter on gas alone, while an insulated *blok* on district heating often costs half that. If the heating advances were set after a mild year and the winter turns out cold, a dopłata is almost guaranteed.

Add tariffs to the mix: Polish energy prices have changed repeatedly in recent years, and settlements use the rates actually in force — not the ones from the day you signed. That's why the post-winter dopłata is the most common and the largest. Signing a lease in summer? Ask for the bills from the last heating season — it's the only way to see the real winter cost.
